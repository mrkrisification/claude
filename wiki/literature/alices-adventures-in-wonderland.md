# Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

Lewis Carroll's 1865 novel follows Alice, a curious young girl who tumbles down a rabbit hole into Wonderland — a surreal realm populated by anthropomorphic animals and absurd characters. The story explores identity, logic, language, and the arbitrary nature of authority, all through the lens of a child's bewildered perspective.

## Plot Summary

Alice is sitting bored by a riverbank when she spots a White Rabbit checking a pocket watch and muttering about being late. She follows it down a rabbit hole and falls into a long underground hall with locked doors. She discovers a tiny golden key that fits a small door leading to a beautiful garden, but cannot fit through it. A bottle labelled "DRINK ME" shrinks her; a cake labelled "EAT ME" grows her — but she repeatedly forgets the key or the wrong size. Through a pool of her own tears, she swims ashore with a Mouse, a Duck, a Dodo, and other creatures.

The story progresses through a series of encounters:
- **Caucus-Race** — the Dodo runs everyone in a circle to get dry; "Everybody has won, and all must have prizes."
- **The White Rabbit's house** — Alice drinks another bottle, grows enormous, fills the house; a lizard named Bill is kicked up the chimney.
- **The Caterpillar** — smoking a hookah on a mushroom, asks "Who are *you*?"; gives Alice pieces of mushroom to control her size.
- **The Duchess's kitchen** — overloaded with pepper; the Duchess's baby turns into a pig; the Cheshire Cat appears and vanishes.
- **Mad Tea-Party** — the Hatter, March Hare, and Dormouse at a permanent tea party, because the Hatter offended Time and it is always six o'clock.
- **The Queen's Croquet-Ground** — croquet played with flamingo mallets and hedgehog balls; the Queen perpetually orders "Off with their heads!"
- **Mock Turtle and Gryphon** — the Mock Turtle describes an undersea school teaching "Reeling and Writhing" and the Lobster Quadrille.
- **Trial of the Knave of Hearts** — accused of stealing tarts; Alice, now grown to full size, defies the Queen; the cards fly at her and she wakes from the dream.

## Key Characters

| Character | Role |
|---|---|
| Alice | Curious, logical, self-possessed girl; the reader's guide through Wonderland |
| White Rabbit | Catalyst; perpetually late, fearful of the Duchess |
| Cheshire Cat | Philosophical guide; disappears leaving only its grin |
| Mad Hatter | Frozen in time at tea; famous for "Why is a raven like a writing-desk?" |
| Queen of Hearts | Authoritarian ruler; "Off with their heads!" is her answer to everything |
| Caterpillar | Dismissive philosopher; teaches Alice to use the mushroom |
| Mock Turtle | Melancholy; parodies Victorian school subjects |

## Major Themes

**Identity and transformation** — Alice constantly changes size and questions who she is. "Who are you?" is the Caterpillar's central challenge. Her sense of self remains stable even as her body does not.

**Language and logic** — Carroll plays with logical inversions throughout: "I mean what I say" vs. "I say what I mean"; the trial where the verdict is demanded before the evidence. Characters exploit ambiguity to frustrate Alice's attempts at rational conversation.

**Absurd authority** — The Queen's power is theatrical rather than real (the Gryphon notes "they never execute nobody"). Rule 42 ("All persons more than a mile high to leave the court") is invented on the spot but declared the oldest rule. Carroll satirises arbitrary institutional authority.

**Dream logic** — The narrative follows dream structure: non-sequential, emotionally coherent but factually impossible, ending with the shock of waking.

## Notable Verses and Quotations

- "How doth the little crocodile / Improve his shining tail" (parody of Isaac Watts' "How doth the little busy bee")
- "You are old, Father William" (parody of Southey's "The Old Man's Comforts")
- "Will you walk a little faster?" (the Lobster Quadrille song)
- "We're all mad here."
- "Curiouser and curiouser!"
- "Sentence first—verdict afterwards."

## Publication and Context

- Written by Charles Lutwidge Dodgson under the pen name Lewis Carroll.
- Originally told orally to Alice Liddell (daughter of Oxford dean Henry Liddell) and her sisters during a boating trip on 4 July 1862.
- Published 1865 by Macmillan, with illustrations by John Tenniel.
- Sequel: *Through the Looking-Glass and What Alice Found There* (1871).
- Now in the public domain; Project Gutenberg eBook #11.
